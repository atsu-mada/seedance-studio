Cinematography Shot Language

Use this reference when a user needs director/DP-grade shot design instead of generic cinematic adjectives.

Camera Movement Grammar

  • Locked-off: use for lip-sync, product identity, text, logos, precise VFX, and continuity anchors.
  • Dolly/push-in: use for discovery, realization, intimacy, product reveal.
  • Lateral track: use for travel, procession, choreography, passing foreground layers.
  • Orbit: use for product hero and statuesque subjects; avoid if identity must remain stable from one angle only.
  • Crane/drone: use for scale, arrival, geography, reveal; avoid for dialogue or tiny product text.
  • Handheld: use for realism or tension; keep movement subtle when identity matters.
  • Rack focus: use for attention shift between two anchored objects; avoid stacking with complex camera moves.

Match Transition by Shared Color or Texture Field (technique T1) [field]

Fill the frame with one uniform color or texture, then reveal that the field belongs to a different place or scale. The shared field carries the eye across the change without a hard cut.

  • Pick a field that exists on both sides: white steam and fresh snow, blue sky and blue fabric, golden sand and wheat-colored paper.
  • Hold the field for a readable moment (about 0.5–1 s) so the viewer settles on it before the reveal.
  • Reveal with one motivated move: a pull-back, a tilt, or a focus change. Name the second place's first readable detail (a chimney, a stitch, a shoreline).
  • Keep the field's color and brightness constant through the hand-off; a light change at that moment breaks the match.

The frame is filled with soft white steam. As the camera slowly pulls back, the white turns out to be the snow-covered roof of a miniature model village, tiny chimneys appearing at the edges.

Scale Reveal Pull-Back (technique T2) [field]

Start on an extreme close-up of a surface and pull back in one continuous move until the whole object reads. It works as a story reveal (small becomes vast) and pairs well with T1.

  • Use one continuous pull-back with steady speed, or a gentle ease-out at the end; no cuts and no direction changes.
  • Name two or three scale landmarks in order, so the model has intermediate states: surface texture, then a region's outline, then the whole object against its surroundings.
  • State the end frame: the whole object, where it sits in frame, and what surrounds it.
  • Budget time: a room-scale or landscape-scale reveal needs most of a clip; do not stack another action on top of it.

Extreme close-up on the woven threads of a patchwork quilt. Single continuous pull-back, steady speed: one patch, then the whole quilt, then the quilt on a bed in a sunlit attic room. Ends with the bed centered, window light falling across it. No cuts.

Blocking and Coverage

For multi-shot scenes, keep camera grammar motivated:

  1. Establish geography and screen direction.
  2. Move to character/product action.
  3. Use close detail only when the detail changes the story.
  4. End each clip with a frame that can become the next first-frame reference.

Coverage pattern:

Shot 1 wide establishes [space/screen direction]. Shot 2 medium follows [action]. Shot 3 close-up reveals [consequence]. Continuity: same wardrobe, prop, light direction, and eyeline.

Professional Avoid List

Avoid: cinematic camera, dynamic shot, epic zoom, film look, Hollywood style, beautiful composition without physical shot choices.

Replace with: shot size, support, movement, subject relation, motivated light, endpoint, and fragile anchors.

POV・寄り・身体断片

匿名の例：POVでは見ている通路と手前の物体を中心にし、見えない全身を無条件に参照しない。顔の寄りでは視線の相手と焦点を固定する。手や肩の断片では該当部位、接触する物体、動作の終点を指定する。画角・合焦対象・可視部位に必要な参照だけを選び、全ショットへ同じ被写界深度を押し付けない。
